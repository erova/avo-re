Over the last few years, companies have moved from limited AI pilots, to pushing usage as high as possible, to throttling it while hunting for cheaper models. Now many are measuring AI use in performance reviews. I’ve seen this first hand: a new section on AI, added to the review framework as part of a broader AI strategy.

Mandates like this, announced without thinking through the second- and third-order effects, make anxiety spike. And the anxiety isn’t mainly about being replaced. In Lenny Rachitsky’s 2026 survey of tech workers, 22% worried about losing their job to AI, a real number but not the top fear. More worried about doing more work for the same pay (51%), an unsustainable pace (46%), and declining quality (41%). Those fears come from an undefined, ever-rising bar for what a designer told to “use AI” is supposed to deliver.

It’s tempting to dismiss this the way my running coach does when I miss my intensity targets: stop whining and get more comfortable being uncomfortable. But discomfort isn’t the problem. The problem is a leadership gap. As of May 2026, only 25% of U.S. employees said their organization had communicated a clear plan for integrating AI. “Just use AI” isn’t a plan. So what is? And what does it mean for each person on the team?

The same survey found that founders are the happiest people in tech, and the likeliest reason is control over their own destiny. Designers don’t need to become founders to get some of that. They need leaders who define what good looks like, give them a say in how they get there, and show them a path forward. Here’s how I’ve tried to do that.

If Use AI is vague and ambiguous, let’s address that head on. The only way to the other side is through—not around, up or over. We define what it means to use AI across levels and situations.

Let’s take one step at a time. First imagine the primary criteria you expect to measure a junior designer off of, from discovery & ideation through delivery and communication. How do you, as their manager or the leader of their organization, expect them to use AI in these themes?

This isn’t just for design, either. Partnering with your peer leadership teams in Product and Engineering and encouraging them to build similar matrices makes everyone feel connected to one standard. The goal posts don’t move, either within Design or across the product development organization.

Agency also applies to how teams will work together. There are no shortage of articles and research indicating disciplines are blurring, that skills matter than titles, and that role clarity may be a vestige of a recently bygone era (and by bygone era I mean *checks watch*  a few years go at best.

Designers can’t throw up their hands and rue the day a PM produced a prototype in an afternoon that would have taken weeks in Figma. That day has come and gone. What reduces the anxiety isn’t pretending it didn’t happen. It’s agreeing, as a team, on how we engage, collaborate, and build together now that anyone can make something that looks real. In other words, a playbook.

First: every prototype is a provocation, not a product. Prototypes are cheap to make and expensive to get attached to. The real skill in AI prototyping isn’t generating the thing. It’s being intentional about what the prototype needs to prove, then spending your time on those details and nothing else. Polish everything and people assume it’s further along than it is. Skimp on the critical interactions and the prototype can’t resolve the ambiguity it was built to resolve, so the team never connects it to the why. That gap between what a prototype looks like and what it’s meant to answer is where the anxiety lives: the feeling of having slipped the dock and drifting toward the horizon, busy but unsure where you’re headed.

Second: everyone knows where prototypes live and who’s seen them. AI prototyping can’t happen in secret. When prototypes live in someone’s DMs, designers learn about a direction after it’s already been decided, and nothing feeds anxiety like finding out late. So make prototypes shareable by default. Critique them in public, not in side conversations. Let people fork a prototype and build on it rather than starting over. None of this should add process. The tools can carry the load: ask your LLM to draft a short markdown file alongside each prototype covering who it’s for, what it’s meant to test, what changed along the way, and what you’d measure if it ever made it to production. Five minutes of context saves five meetings.

Third: know where the gains are, and where they aren’t. AI speeds up the early work dramatically: discovery, ideation, first drafts. Then the gains thin out in the last mile, in final design and handoff, where the details and edge cases live. Plan around the upstream gains and you’ll promise timelines the team can’t hit, which is exactly how designers end up doing more work for the same pay at an unsustainable pace. Plan for net, not gross.

## Show the way forward

Clear expectations and a shared playbook answer the question “what do you want from me today?” They don’t answer the one that keeps people up at night: “what happens to me next year?”

Start with an honest picture of where the team stands. I use a readiness model that looks at six systems: the design system, AI and tech infrastructure, workflow, how the triad holds itself accountable, track record to base future predictions on past results, and whether customers are ready for what we ship (I work in governance, risk, and compliance, which means our corporate users are pretty risk-averse). The rule that matters is that the weakest system sets the ceiling. The most AI-pilled designer can’t prompt their way past a design system that isn’t ready for AI, and they shouldn’t be judged as if they could.

Then make the career path map to the work. Look back at the tables: production drops from 35% of a junior designer’s expectations to nothing at staff, while judgment, influence, and ownership climb. You’re laying out a path that reflects a progression, and it should show up in how you hire and promote. Your teams will also recognize what they have to do to get to the next level instead of believing promotions are arbitrary or based on who goes to happy hour with the boss. When designers see that the skills AI can’t replace are the ones that get rewarded, the future looks less like a cliff and more like a staircase heading up (though in this world it may be an MC Escher-like staircase).

## The tools won’t be the difference

Here’s what I keep coming back to. Within a few years, the tools will be roughly the same everywhere. Every team will have the same models, the same prototyping tools, the same agents. When the tools stop being the difference, the difference will be how intentionally an org leads and works: whether people know what’s expected of them, how they build together, and where they’re headed. The orgs that chase every new tool and feature will stay chaotic. The ones that invest in their people will compound.

That isn’t a soft conclusion. In Lenny’s survey, manager effectiveness was the strongest driver of burnout, stronger than role, company size, or how people felt about AI. The biggest lever to reduce your team’s anxiety isn’t another tool and it probably doesn’t burn tokens. It’s how you operate and show up as a leader.

We’re not done. My team is further along than we’d have been without this work, and some weeks it still feels like we’re making this up as we go along, or can find ourselves surprised and reacting off our back foot. “Use AI” means something now, and it also means we have a clear path forward and a map to navigate us on our journey.
